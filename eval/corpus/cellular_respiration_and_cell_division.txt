Cellular respiration, mitosis, and meiosis

PART ONE: CELLULAR RESPIRATION

Cellular respiration is the set of reactions by which cells extract energy from food molecules and store it in a form that can be used for work. The most common fuel is glucose. In the presence of oxygen, one molecule of glucose is broken down to carbon dioxide and water, and the energy released is used to make adenosine triphosphate, or ATP, the main energy currency of the cell. In its simplest form the overall reaction is the reverse of photosynthesis: glucose plus oxygen gives carbon dioxide plus water plus energy. Respiration occurs in nearly all living cells, in plants as well as animals, and it happens day and night. Plants do not rely on photosynthesis alone, because the sugars they make must still be burned in respiration to power growth and transport.

Respiration has four main stages: glycolysis, pyruvate oxidation, the citric acid cycle, and oxidative phosphorylation. The first stage takes place in the cytosol, the fluid part of the cell outside the organelles, while the last three take place inside the mitochondria. This division of labor reflects the evolutionary history of the mitochondrion, which is thought to descend from a free-living bacterium that was engulfed by an ancestor of modern cells. Mitochondria are surrounded by two membranes, have their own small circular DNA, and divide independently of the cell. The inner membrane is folded into ridges called cristae, which increase the surface area available for the electron transport machinery. The fluid inside the inner membrane is called the matrix.

Glycolysis means the splitting of sugar. It is a sequence of ten enzyme-catalyzed steps that converts one six-carbon glucose molecule into two three-carbon molecules of pyruvate. The first step is carried out by the enzyme hexokinase, which adds a phosphate group to glucose and traps it inside the cell. The step that is most important for regulation is catalyzed by phosphofructokinase, an enzyme that is slowed down when ATP is abundant and sped up when ATP is scarce, so that the cell burns sugar in proportion to its need. The early steps use up two ATP molecules as an investment, and the later steps produce four ATP, so the net gain is two ATP per glucose. Glycolysis also produces two molecules of NADH, a carrier of high-energy electrons. It does not require oxygen, which makes it the oldest and most universal energy-releasing pathway.

When oxygen is available, pyruvate is carried into the mitochondrial matrix. There the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex removes one carbon as carbon dioxide and attaches the remaining two-carbon fragment to a carrier called coenzyme A, producing acetyl-CoA. This step also generates one NADH for each pyruvate, so two NADH per glucose. It is sometimes called the link reaction because it connects glycolysis to the citric acid cycle.

The citric acid cycle is also called the Krebs cycle, after Hans Krebs, who worked out its steps in 1937. It takes place in the mitochondrial matrix. Acetyl-CoA joins with a four-carbon molecule, oxaloacetate, to form the six-carbon citrate; the reaction is catalyzed by the enzyme citrate synthase. Through a series of rearrangements the citrate loses two carbons as carbon dioxide, and oxaloacetate is regenerated so that the cycle can turn again. For each turn of the cycle, the cell makes three NADH, one FADH2, and one molecule of ATP or GTP. Since each glucose molecule yields two acetyl-CoA, the cycle turns twice per glucose, producing six NADH, two FADH2, and two ATP in total. By the end of the cycle all six carbons of the original glucose have left the cell as carbon dioxide.

Most of the ATP is made in the final stage, oxidative phosphorylation, which takes place at the inner mitochondrial membrane. NADH and FADH2 deliver their electrons to a chain of protein complexes, labeled complex I through complex IV, embedded in the membrane. As electrons pass from one complex to the next, the complexes pump protons out of the matrix into the space between the two membranes. This builds up a gradient, with a high concentration of protons outside and a low concentration inside. At the end of the chain the electrons are handed to molecular oxygen, which is the final electron acceptor, and combine with protons to form water. That is the reason humans need to breathe oxygen.

The proton gradient stores energy like water behind a dam. The protons flow back into the matrix through a rotary enzyme called ATP synthase, and the flow drives the enzyme to attach phosphate to ADP and make ATP. This coupling of electron transport to ATP synthesis through a proton gradient is known as chemiosmosis, an idea proposed by Peter Mitchell in 1961, for which he received the Nobel Prize in 1978. A very similar mechanism operates in the thylakoid membranes of chloroplasts during photosynthesis, but in chloroplasts the gradient is built with energy from light, and the ATP synthase releases ATP into the stroma.

The total ATP yield from one glucose molecule is about 30 to 32. Older textbooks gave a figure of 36 or 38, but later measurements showed that the cost of moving ATP and other molecules across the mitochondrial membranes reduces the net gain. The tally is roughly 2 ATP from glycolysis, 2 from the citric acid cycle, and the remainder from oxidative phosphorylation, which accounts for about 26 to 28 of the total. Each NADH is worth about 2.5 ATP and each FADH2 about 1.5 ATP.

When oxygen is not available, cells cannot run the electron transport chain, and NADH builds up. To keep glycolysis going, cells use fermentation, which regenerates the NAD+ that glycolysis needs but yields only the 2 ATP from glycolysis. In animals, including humans during intense exercise, pyruvate is converted to lactate by the enzyme lactate dehydrogenase. In yeast, pyruvate is first converted to acetaldehyde by pyruvate decarboxylase, releasing carbon dioxide, and then to ethanol by alcohol dehydrogenase. This is the basis for brewing and the rising of bread dough. Fermentation is much less efficient than aerobic respiration, because it extracts only a small fraction of the energy in glucose.

Respiration is tightly regulated. High levels of ATP and NADH slow the key enzymes of glycolysis and the citric acid cycle, while high levels of ADP speed them up. Fats and proteins can also be burned: fatty acids are chopped into two-carbon pieces that enter the cycle as acetyl-CoA, which is why a gram of fat yields more than twice the energy of a gram of carbohydrate.

PART TWO: MITOSIS

Cells grow and reproduce through a regular sequence of events called the cell cycle. In a typical human cell the cycle takes about 24 hours. It is divided into interphase and the mitotic phase. Interphase has three parts: G1, during which the cell grows and carries out its normal functions; S phase, during which the DNA is copied; and G2, during which the cell prepares to divide. Mitosis itself, with cytokinesis, makes up only about an hour of the cycle. Progress through the cycle is controlled by checkpoints at the end of G1, at the end of G2, and during mitosis. The proteins that drive the cell through these checkpoints are cyclins and cyclin-dependent kinases. The tumor suppressor protein p53 can halt the cycle when DNA is damaged, and when this control fails the result can be cancer.

After the S phase each chromosome consists of two identical sister chromatids joined at a region called the centromere. Mitosis separates these chromatids into two daughter nuclei, and it has four classic stages, which students often remember with the abbreviation PMAT. In prophase the chromosomes condense and become visible, the nuclear envelope breaks down, and the spindle begins to form from structures called centrosomes. In metaphase the chromosomes line up along the middle of the cell, called the metaphase plate, and spindle fibers attach to a protein structure at each centromere called the kinetochore. In anaphase the sister chromatids are pulled apart and move to opposite poles, each now counted as a separate chromosome. In telophase two new nuclear envelopes form, the chromosomes uncoil, and the spindle disappears.

Cytokinesis, the division of the cytoplasm, usually begins during late anaphase or telophase. In animal cells a ring of protein filaments tightens like a drawstring and pinches the cell in two, forming a cleavage furrow. In plant cells there is a rigid wall, so instead a cell plate forms in the middle of the cell, grows outward, and becomes a new wall between the two daughter cells.

The result of mitosis is two daughter cells that are genetically identical to the parent and to each other. A human body cell has 46 chromosomes, arranged in 23 pairs, which is called the diploid number. After mitosis, each daughter cell also has 46 chromosomes. Mitosis is used for growth, for repair of tissue, and for asexual reproduction in many single-celled organisms. It involves one round of nuclear division.

PART THREE: MEIOSIS

Meiosis is a different kind of cell division that makes sex cells, called gametes. In humans these are sperm and eggs. Meiosis halves the number of chromosomes, so that when a sperm fertilizes an egg, the full number is restored in the offspring. A human gamete has 23 chromosomes, which is the haploid number. Unlike mitosis, meiosis involves two successive divisions, called meiosis I and meiosis II, with only one round of DNA replication before them. One diploid cell therefore ends up as four haploid cells.

In meiosis I, the homologous chromosomes, one from the mother and one from the father, find each other and pair up in prophase I. This pairing is called synapsis, and the resulting group of four chromatids is called a tetrad or bivalent. While paired, the homologous chromosomes swap segments in a process called crossing over, which occurs at points called chiasmata. Crossing over shuffles the genes between maternal and paternal chromosomes and is a major source of genetic variety. In metaphase I the tetrads line up along the middle of the cell in pairs, not as single chromosomes as in mitosis. In anaphase I the homologous chromosomes are separated and pulled to opposite poles, but the sister chromatids stay joined. The cell then divides into two cells that are each haploid, although each chromosome still has two chromatids.

Meiosis II resembles mitosis. The chromosomes line up singly in metaphase II, and in anaphase II the sister chromatids finally separate. After the second division there are four haploid cells, each with 23 single chromosomes in humans. Because of crossing over and the random way the homologous pairs line up at metaphase I, which is called independent assortment, each of the four cells is genetically different. With 23 pairs of chromosomes, independent assortment alone can produce more than 8 million combinations, about 2 to the power of 23.

Errors in meiosis can cause serious conditions. If chromosomes fail to separate properly, a phenomenon called nondisjunction, a gamete may end up with an extra or missing chromosome. The most familiar example is Down syndrome, which usually results from three copies of chromosome 21. The risk increases with the age of the mother because human eggs remain paused in meiosis for decades.

There are also differences between the production of sperm and eggs. In males, spermatogenesis produces four functional sperm cells from each cell that enters meiosis. In females, oogenesis divides the cytoplasm unequally, so each cell entering meiosis yields one large egg and two or three tiny cells called polar bodies, which degenerate. The egg is arrested at prophase I before birth and completes meiosis II only if it is fertilized.

A direct comparison of mitosis and meiosis is a favorite exam topic. Mitosis has one division, while meiosis has two. Mitosis produces two cells, while meiosis produces four. Mitotic daughter cells are diploid and identical, while meiotic cells are haploid and different from each other. Crossing over and synapsis happen only in meiosis. Mitosis occurs in body cells, and meiosis occurs only in the reproductive organs. In both processes the DNA is copied just once, in the S phase of interphase.

Compare also the locations of events in respiration with those in photosynthesis. Glycolysis happens in the cytosol, the citric acid cycle happens in the mitochondrial matrix, and the electron transport chain is in the inner mitochondrial membrane. In photosynthesis, the light reactions happen in the thylakoid membranes and the Calvin cycle takes place in the stroma. The enzyme at the heart of respiration's final step is ATP synthase, while the signature enzyme of the Calvin cycle is RuBisCO.

ATP itself is a small molecule made of adenine, the sugar ribose, and three phosphate groups. The energy is stored in the bond between the second and third phosphates. When the cell needs energy, an enzyme called an ATPase removes the last phosphate, producing ADP and releasing about 30 kilojoules per mole. A typical person turns over roughly their own body weight in ATP every day, because each molecule is used and recycled many times, each one thousands of times per day. Muscles also hold a small reserve of creatine phosphate, which can quickly regenerate ATP during the first seconds of a sprint.

During hard exercise the muscle cells may use oxygen faster than it can be delivered, and they switch partly to lactic acid fermentation. The lactate that builds up was once blamed for sore muscles, but later research showed that it is cleared fairly quickly and is itself used as a fuel by the heart and other tissues. After a hard effort, breathing stays rapid because the body must pay back an oxygen debt, which is the extra oxygen needed to restore ATP, creatine phosphate, and normal metabolism. Athletes who train for endurance have more mitochondria in their muscle cells, which gives them a larger capacity for aerobic respiration.

Poisons often act on the respiratory machinery. Cyanide and carbon monoxide bind to complex IV of the electron transport chain and stop it from passing electrons to oxygen, which halts ATP production and kills cells within minutes. The compound 2,4-dinitrophenol makes the inner membrane leaky to protons, so energy from the gradient is lost as heat rather than used to make ATP. Brown fat tissue does this deliberately with a protein called thermogenin, or UCP1, to keep newborn babies and hibernating animals warm. Inherited defects in mitochondrial DNA cause diseases that mainly affect organs with high energy demands, such as the brain, heart, and muscle. Mitochondrial DNA is inherited from the mother, because the egg contributes nearly all of the mitochondria to the embryo.

A bookkeeping summary helps to compare the stages. Glycolysis, in the cytosol, nets 2 ATP and 2 NADH per glucose. Pyruvate oxidation, in the matrix, yields 2 NADH. The citric acid cycle, also in the matrix, gives 2 ATP, 6 NADH, and 2 FADH2. Altogether this makes 10 NADH and 2 FADH2 per glucose, which feed the electron transport chain at the inner membrane. Carbon dioxide is released at two places, the pyruvate oxidation step and the citric acid cycle, and oxygen is used only at the very end of the electron transport chain. Water is produced at that last step.

The cell cycle can also go wrong in other ways. Cells that stop dividing permanently enter a state called G0. Nerve cells and most heart muscle cells stay there for life, while liver cells can re-enter the cycle if the liver is damaged. Cancer cells ignore the normal checkpoints and divide without limit, and many chemotherapy drugs work by interfering with the spindle, as taxol does by freezing it in place, or by damaging DNA in cells that divide rapidly. Normal tissues that divide quickly, such as hair follicles and the lining of the gut, are also affected, which explains the side effects of these treatments.

Cells in the body typically divide a limited number of times. The biologist Leonard Hayflick found in 1961 that human fibroblast cells grown in the laboratory divide about 40 to 60 times before stopping. The reason is that the ends of chromosomes, called telomeres, shorten with each division. An enzyme called telomerase can rebuild telomeres, and it is active in stem cells, germ cells, and most cancer cells.

In plants, mitosis is concentrated in regions called meristems, found at the tips of roots and shoots. In a classic school experiment, thin slices of an onion root tip are stained and examined under a microscope to count how many cells are in each phase. Because most cells are found in interphase, it can be estimated that interphase takes up the largest part of the cell cycle, and mitosis is brief.
