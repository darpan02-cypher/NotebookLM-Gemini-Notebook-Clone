Apollo missions in detail: from the first crewed flight to the final lunar landing

This document walks through the individual Apollo flights one at a time. It complements the general overview of the program by giving the dates, crews, spacecraft names, landing sites and durations that distinguish each mission from the others.

Apollo 7 was the first crewed flight of the Apollo spacecraft. It lifted off on October 11, 1968, and stayed in Earth orbit until splashdown on October 22, 1968, a flight of roughly ten days and twenty hours. The crew was Wally Schirra, Donn Eisele, and Walter Cunningham. Because the rocket was the smaller Saturn IB, the mission never left low Earth orbit. Its purpose was to prove that the command and service module could keep a crew alive for a long flight and that its main engine could be restarted many times. Schirra caught a bad head cold during the flight, and the crew grew irritable and argued with mission control about the schedule, which is why none of the three ever flew in space again. The mission did include the first live television broadcasts from an American crewed spacecraft.

Apollo 8 followed in December 1968 and was a bold change of plan. It launched on December 21, 1968, aboard a Saturn V and carried Frank Borman, Jim Lovell, and William Anders to the Moon without a lunar module. The crew became the first humans to travel to the Moon and the first to see the far side of it with their own eyes. They completed ten orbits of the Moon over about twenty hours. On the fourth orbit Anders photographed the Earth rising above the lunar horizon, an image that became known as Earthrise. On Christmas Eve the crew read verses from the Book of Genesis in a television broadcast watched by a very large audience. Apollo 8 returned to Earth on December 27, 1968, splashing down in the Pacific Ocean.

Apollo 9 flew from March 3 to March 13, 1969, and tested the lunar module for the first time with a crew aboard. The astronauts were James McDivitt, David Scott, and Russell Schweickart, and the whole flight stayed in Earth orbit. The command module was named Gumdrop and the lunar module was named Spider. Because the lunar module had spindly legs and no aerodynamic shape, it could only fly in the vacuum of space, and the name Spider seemed fitting. During the mission the crew separated the two spacecraft, flew them apart by about 180 kilometers, and then rendezvoused and docked again using the lunar module's own engines. Schweickart performed a spacewalk to test the portable life support backpack that the Moon walkers would later use.

Apollo 10 was the full dress rehearsal for the landing. It flew from May 18 to May 26, 1969, with Thomas Stafford, John Young, and Eugene Cernan aboard. The command module was called Charlie Brown and the lunar module was called Snoopy. Stafford and Cernan took Snoopy down to an altitude of about 15.6 kilometers above the lunar surface, close enough to photograph the planned Apollo 11 landing site in the Sea of Tranquility, while Young remained in orbit in the command module. Planners deliberately loaded the lunar module with too little fuel to land and take off again, so that the crew could not be tempted to try. On the way home, Apollo 10 reached a speed of nearly 40,000 kilometers per hour, still the highest speed ever reached by humans relative to Earth.

Apollo 12 carried out the second lunar landing. It launched on November 14, 1969, in rain, and the Saturn V was struck by lightning twice during the first minute of flight. Several instruments went dark, but a flight controller suggested switching a signal conditioning unit to its auxiliary setting, the crew did so, and the mission continued. The crew was Charles "Pete" Conrad, Alan Bean, and Richard Gordon. Conrad and Bean landed the lunar module Intrepid in the Ocean of Storms on November 19, 1969, while Gordon orbited in the command module Yankee Clipper. The landing was a precision one: the lunar module touched down only about 180 meters from the robotic Surveyor 3 probe, which had landed in 1967. The two astronauts walked to Surveyor 3 and removed pieces of it, including its camera, so that engineers could study how the metal had aged on the Moon. They made two moonwalks totaling about seven hours and forty-five minutes and brought back roughly 34.3 kilograms of samples. Apollo 12 splashed down on November 24, 1969.

Apollo 13 is covered in the overview of the program, so it is mentioned here only to explain why the next landing mission was Apollo 14. After the explosion on Apollo 13, engineers spent months redesigning the oxygen tanks and adding a third tank and an extra battery to the service module before flights could resume.

Apollo 14 launched on January 31, 1971, and landed on February 5, 1971. The commander was Alan Shepard, who had been the first American in space in 1961, and he was joined by Edgar Mitchell, who walked on the Moon with him, and Stuart Roosa, who remained in orbit aboard the command module Kitty Hawk. The lunar module was named Antares. The target was the Fra Mauro highlands, the area that Apollo 13 had been supposed to visit. The two moonwalks lasted about nine hours and twenty-three minutes in total. During the second one, the astronauts hauled a two-wheeled cart for tools and samples and tried to reach the rim of Cone Crater, but the featureless terrain made it hard to judge distances and they turned back short of the rim. At the end Shepard smuggled a golf club head onto the surface and hit two golf balls. The crew returned about 42.3 kilograms of rock and soil. Apollo 14 splashed down on February 9, 1971.

Apollo 15 was the first of the so-called J missions, which carried more equipment and stayed longer on the surface. It launched on July 26, 1971, with David Scott, James Irwin, and Alfred Worden. Scott and Irwin landed the lunar module Falcon on July 30, 1971, at the foot of the Apennine mountains beside Hadley Rille, while Worden orbited in the command module Endeavour. For the first time the astronauts drove a lunar roving vehicle, which let them range several kilometers from the lander. The surface stay lasted almost three days and included three moonwalks totaling about eighteen and a half hours. Near Spur crater they found a pale rock rich in the mineral plagioclase, nicknamed the Genesis Rock, which is about four billion years old and was thought to be a piece of the original lunar crust. The crew collected about 77 kilograms of samples. Scott also dropped a hammer and a falcon feather together on camera to show that they fall at the same rate without air. Apollo 15 splashed down on August 7, 1971.

Apollo 16 launched on April 16, 1972, carrying John Young, Charles Duke, and Thomas Kenneth "Ken" Mattingly. Young and Duke landed the lunar module Orion on April 21, 1972, in the Descartes Highlands, the first landing in the rugged lunar highlands rather than on a plain. Mattingly stayed in orbit in the command module Casper. Scientists had expected to find volcanic rock at Descartes, but the samples turned out to be mostly breccias formed by impacts, which overturned a popular theory. The astronauts made three moonwalks totaling about twenty hours and fourteen minutes and returned about 95.7 kilograms of lunar material, the second largest haul of the program. Duke left a family photograph on the surface. Mattingly, who had been removed from Apollo 13 days before launch because of possible measles exposure, never actually got the disease and flew on Apollo 16 instead. He performed a spacewalk on the way home to retrieve film cassettes from the service module. Apollo 16 splashed down on April 27, 1972.

Apollo 17 was the final mission of the program and the only one to carry a professional scientist to the surface. The crew was Eugene Cernan, geologist Harrison "Jack" Schmitt, and Ronald Evans. It launched on December 7, 1972, at night, the only night launch of a Saturn V, lighting up the sky for hundreds of kilometers. Cernan and Schmitt landed the lunar module Challenger on December 11, 1972, in the Taurus-Littrow valley, while Evans remained in the command module America. They made three moonwalks totaling about twenty-two hours, the longest of the program, and they drove the roving vehicle on traverses that carried them several kilometers from the lander. They discovered orange soil near Shorty crater, which proved to be tiny glass beads produced by ancient volcanic fire fountains. The crew brought back about 110.5 kilograms of samples, the largest amount of any mission. As the astronauts left, Cernan spoke of America's return to the Moon someday. The whole flight lasted about twelve days and fourteen hours, and the crew splashed down on December 19, 1972. While the spacecraft was on its way to the Moon, the crew took a photograph of the whole sunlit Earth that became known as the Blue Marble.

Several patterns appear when the missions are compared. Three astronauts flew to the Moon twice: Jim Lovell on Apollo 8 and Apollo 13, John Young on Apollo 10 and Apollo 16, and Eugene Cernan on Apollo 10 and Apollo 17. Young and Cernan each flew a rehearsal mission first and then a landing mission, while Lovell never landed. Each lunar module name was chosen by its crew: Eagle, Intrepid, Antares, Falcon, Orion, and Challenger were the names of the six that landed. The command modules for those landings were Columbia, Yankee Clipper, Kitty Hawk, Endeavour, Casper, and America.

The landing sites also form a progression of increasing difficulty. Apollo 11 touched down on a smooth mare in the Sea of Tranquility. Apollo 12 pinpointed a spot in the Ocean of Storms. Apollo 14 went to the Fra Mauro highlands. Apollo 15 chose a mountain valley with a rille. Apollo 16 targeted the Descartes Highlands. Apollo 17 settled in the Taurus-Littrow valley between mountain massifs. As the missions advanced, the time on the surface grew from about twenty-one hours for Apollo 11 to about seventy-five hours for Apollo 17, and the mass of samples returned climbed from about 21.5 kilograms to about 110.5 kilograms.

Engineers also improved the equipment. The first two landing crews walked everywhere. Apollo 14 introduced the hand-pulled cart, which was called the modular equipment transporter. Apollo 15, 16, and 17 each carried a battery-powered rover, and the extended command module bays carried cameras and instruments that mapped the Moon from orbit. The service module of the J missions had a scientific instrument bay holding cameras, a laser altimeter and a spectrometer, and the command module pilot tended it during the days alone in orbit.

Budget pressure ended the program earlier than planned. NASA had originally intended to fly Apollo 18, 19, and 20, but Congress cut the budget and the agency canceled them in 1970, partly because the Saturn V production line was winding down and leftover hardware was reused for the Skylab space station. Leftover hardware found a new use: the Saturn V that had been built for a later mission launched the Skylab space station in 1973, and the unused rockets were eventually put on public display.

Mission durations and surface times can be summarized as follows. Apollo 7 lasted about 10 days and 20 hours. Apollo 8, the first lunar voyage, lasted only about 6 days and 3 hours. Apollo 9 lasted about 10 days, and Apollo 10 about 8 days. Among the landing missions, Apollo 12 lasted about 10 days and 4 hours, Apollo 14 about 9 days, Apollo 15 about 12 days and 7 hours, Apollo 16 about 11 days and 2 hours, and Apollo 17 about 12 days and 14 hours. The time that the lunar module spent on the surface rose steadily: about 31 hours and 31 minutes for Apollo 12, about 33 hours and 31 minutes for Apollo 14, about 67 hours for Apollo 15, about 71 hours for Apollo 16, and about 75 hours for Apollo 17.

The hardware that carried these crews was upgraded along the way. The first landing missions used a lunar module that could support two men for roughly two days on the surface. The extended lunar module flown on Apollo 15 to 17 had larger propellant tanks, more batteries, and extra oxygen and water, which made the three-day stays possible. The suits changed too: the suits for Apollo 15 to 17 had a more flexible waist joint so that the astronauts could bend and sit on the rover.

Crews trained for geology in the field. Before Apollo 15, 16, and 17, the astronauts took many field trips to places such as the volcanic fields of Hawaii, the Meteor Crater in Arizona, and the volcanic terrain of Iceland, learning to describe rocks and decide quickly which ones were worth collecting. On the Moon each sample was photographed in place before being bagged, so that scientists could later link every rock to the exact spot where it was found. Samples were sent to the Lunar Receiving Laboratory in Houston, where the first crews and their rocks were quarantined for about three weeks in case they carried unknown lunar organisms; the quarantine was dropped after Apollo 14 when no evidence of any hazard had turned up.

The instruments that astronauts left behind formed a network of scientific stations. The Apollo Lunar Surface Experiments Package carried by Apollo 12, 14, 15, 16, and 17 included seismometers, magnetometers, and heat-flow probes. The Apollo 11 crew had left a smaller set of experiments that ran for a few weeks, but the later stations ran on nuclear power and transmitted data for years until they were switched off on September 30, 1977. Seismometers recorded thousands of moonquakes and the impacts of meteoroids, and they showed that the Moon has a crust, a mantle, and a small core. Retroreflectors left by Apollo 11, 14, and 15 are still used to bounce laser beams from Earth and measure the distance to the Moon to within a few millimeters. The Moon is moving away from the Earth at about 3.8 centimeters per year, a result learned from these measurements.

Taken together, the Apollo samples gave strong support to the giant impact theory for the origin of the Moon, in which a Mars-sized body struck the early Earth and the debris collected into the Moon. The rocks from Apollo 12 and Apollo 15 helped establish that the dark maria are ancient lava flows, and radioactive dating showed that the ages of the mare rocks range from about 3.1 to 3.8 billion years. The highland rocks from Apollo 16 and Apollo 17 are older, up to about 4.4 billion years.
