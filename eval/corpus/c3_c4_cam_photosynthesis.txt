Three ways to fix carbon: C3, C4, and CAM photosynthesis

All green plants use the Calvin cycle to turn carbon dioxide into sugar, but they differ in how carbon dioxide reaches the cycle. Plants are grouped into three types according to the way they handle this first step: C3, C4, and CAM. The names refer to the number of carbon atoms in the first stable product, or in the case of CAM, to Crassulacean acid metabolism, a pathway first studied in members of the stonecrop family, Crassulaceae. The differences are mostly adaptations to heat, bright light, and water shortage, and they help to explain why certain plants dominate certain climates.

C3 photosynthesis is the original and most common pathway. About 85 percent of plant species use it, including rice, wheat, barley, soybeans, potatoes, and nearly all trees. In a C3 plant, carbon dioxide that enters through the stomata is picked up directly by the enzyme RuBisCO in the mesophyll cells of the leaf. RuBisCO attaches the carbon dioxide to a five-carbon sugar called ribulose bisphosphate, and the resulting six-carbon compound immediately splits into two molecules of the three-carbon acid 3-phosphoglycerate. Because this is the first stable product and has three carbons, the pathway is called C3. All of the reactions of the Calvin cycle occur in the stroma of the chloroplasts in the same cells.

C3 plants have a weakness, which is that RuBisCO can also react with oxygen instead of carbon dioxide. When it does, the plant wastes energy and releases carbon dioxide in a process called photorespiration. The tendency for oxygen to compete with carbon dioxide rises with temperature, because warm leaves hold less dissolved carbon dioxide relative to oxygen, and because plants close their stomata on hot days, which causes carbon dioxide inside the leaf to fall and oxygen to build up. In hot weather, photorespiration can cut the efficiency of a C3 plant by a quarter or more. C3 plants therefore do best in cool, moist climates with moderate light, with an optimum temperature of roughly 15 to 25 degrees Celsius, and their photosynthesis levels off at moderate light intensities.

C4 photosynthesis is an adaptation that largely avoids photorespiration. About 3 percent of plant species are C4 plants, but they account for around a quarter of the primary production on land because they include highly productive grasses such as maize, sugarcane, sorghum, and many tropical pasture grasses. Researchers Marshall Hatch and Charles Slack described the pathway in the mid-1960s, and it is sometimes called the Hatch-Slack pathway.

The key to C4 photosynthesis is a division of labor between two kinds of cells. The leaves of C4 plants have a special wreath-like arrangement known as Kranz anatomy, in which a ring of bundle sheath cells surrounds each vein, and these are in turn surrounded by mesophyll cells. In the mesophyll cells, carbon dioxide is first captured by an enzyme called PEP carboxylase, not by RuBisCO. PEP carboxylase attaches carbon dioxide, in the form of bicarbonate, to a three-carbon molecule called phosphoenolpyruvate, producing the four-carbon acid oxaloacetate. Because the first stable product has four carbons, the pathway is called C4. PEP carboxylase has a high affinity for carbon dioxide and does not react with oxygen, so it works well even when the stomata are partly closed.

The four-carbon acid is converted to malate or aspartate and is moved into the bundle sheath cells, where it is broken down to release carbon dioxide. This pumps up the carbon dioxide concentration around RuBisCO to several times the normal level, so that RuBisCO almost always meets carbon dioxide rather than oxygen. The Calvin cycle then runs in the bundle sheath cells. The three-carbon remnant returns to the mesophyll, where it is converted back to phosphoenolpyruvate. This regeneration costs two extra ATP per carbon dioxide molecule, so a C4 plant uses about five ATP per carbon dioxide fixed, compared with three for a C3 plant. The extra cost pays off in hot, bright conditions, but is a disadvantage in cool, shady ones. C4 plants have an optimum temperature of about 30 to 40 degrees Celsius, and their photosynthesis does not saturate even in full sunlight.

Because C4 plants can fix carbon dioxide efficiently with their stomata only partly open, they lose less water for each gram of dry matter they make. Typical figures are that a C4 plant may use around 250 to 350 grams of water per gram of dry matter, while a C3 plant uses roughly 450 to 950 grams. That is why maize and sugarcane thrive in warm regions where the sun is strong.

CAM photosynthesis is the strategy of plants in very dry environments. About 6 to 7 percent of species use CAM, including cacti, agave, pineapple, many orchids, and succulents such as Kalanchoe and the stonecrops. CAM plants do not separate the two steps in space, as C4 plants do, but in time. At night, when the air is cool and humid, the stomata open. Carbon dioxide enters the leaf and is captured by PEP carboxylase, forming oxaloacetate, which is converted to malate. The malate is stored as malic acid in the large central vacuole of the cell. As a result the cells become measurably more acidic by morning, which is a hallmark of CAM.

During the day, the stomata close tightly to prevent loss of water. The malate leaves the vacuole and is broken down to release carbon dioxide inside the cell, where RuBisCO and the Calvin cycle use it with the help of ATP and NADPH from the light reactions, which operate in sunlight. This allows the plant to photosynthesize with its stomata shut during the heat of the day. CAM plants typically lose only a small amount of water, in the range of about 50 to 100 grams for each gram of dry matter. The price is slow growth, because the amount of carbon dioxide that can be stored in the vacuole at night is limited. This is why cacti and agaves grow slowly compared with maize.

A comparison of the three pathways is useful. The first enzyme to capture carbon dioxide is RuBisCO in C3 plants and PEP carboxylase in both C4 and CAM plants. The Calvin cycle occurs in mesophyll cells in C3 plants, in bundle sheath cells in C4 plants, and in the same cells as the night-time capture in CAM plants. The separation of carbon dioxide capture from the Calvin cycle is spatial in C4 plants and temporal in CAM plants. Stomata open during the day in C3 and C4 plants but open at night in CAM plants. The number of carbon atoms in the first product is three for C3 and four for the other two.

Climate and evolution shape the distribution of these plants. C4 photosynthesis evolved independently many times, more than sixty separate times, beginning about 30 million years ago when atmospheric carbon dioxide had declined. The spread of C4 grasslands about 6 to 8 million years ago is thought to have influenced the diets of grazing animals, including early human ancestors. CAM has also evolved many times, and some plants can switch between CAM and C3 depending on the amount of available water.

The stable isotopes of carbon provide a way to tell the three types apart. RuBisCO discriminates against the heavier carbon-13 more than PEP carboxylase does, so C3 plants contain proportionately less carbon-13 than C4 plants, with CAM plants falling somewhere in between. Scientists use this fact to study ancient diets, because the carbon isotope ratio of an animal's bones and teeth reflects whether it ate mostly C3 or C4 plants.

Crop science makes use of these differences. Because C4 crops are so efficient, researchers have tried to engineer the C4 pathway into rice, a C3 crop, in the hope of raising yields by up to half. The project involves reproducing Kranz anatomy or a similar arrangement and has been challenging. Another approach is to modify photorespiration in C3 crops so that the wasteful byproducts are recycled faster, and field trials in tobacco have shown that this can increase growth.

Climate change affects the three types differently. Rising carbon dioxide gives C3 plants a bigger advantage, since it suppresses photorespiration and gives RuBisCO more carbon dioxide to work with, while C4 plants already concentrate carbon dioxide and benefit less. Higher temperatures and drought, on the other hand, tend to favor C4 and CAM plants. In many places the net effect depends on local conditions.

To connect this with the basic process, remember that all three pathways rely on the same light reactions in the thylakoid membranes, where water is split and oxygen is released, and on the Calvin cycle in the stroma of the chloroplast, where RuBisCO assembles sugar from carbon dioxide. The pathways differ only in how carbon dioxide is delivered to RuBisCO. Respiration, by contrast, takes place in the mitochondria and releases carbon dioxide rather than consuming it. In a leaf during the day, the rate of photosynthesis normally far exceeds the rate of respiration, so the plant is a net consumer of carbon dioxide. At night, without light, only respiration continues, and the plant releases carbon dioxide, with the exception of CAM plants, which take in carbon dioxide at night while their stomata are open.

The details of photorespiration show why it is so costly. When RuBisCO attaches oxygen to ribulose bisphosphate, it produces one molecule of 3-phosphoglycerate and one molecule of a two-carbon compound called phosphoglycolate, which is toxic to the plant and cannot enter the Calvin cycle. The plant must salvage the carbon through a series of reactions that occur in three organelles, the chloroplast, the peroxisome, and the mitochondrion. The salvage pathway releases carbon dioxide and consumes ATP, and it loses ammonia that must be recaptured. In a hot dry day the loss can be a large portion of what was just fixed. RuBisCO evolved when the atmosphere contained little oxygen and much more carbon dioxide, which explains why it handles oxygen so poorly. It is also a very slow enzyme, handling only about three molecules of carbon dioxide per second, so plants have to make huge amounts of it. RuBisCO can make up about half of the soluble protein in a leaf.

Examples from agriculture illustrate the differences. Wheat and rice are grown widely in temperate and subtropical regions and rely on C3 photosynthesis. Maize, sugarcane, and sorghum are C4 crops whose yields are among the highest of any cereals per hectare. Pineapple is the best-known CAM crop, and farmers can plant it in dry soil because its leaves lose so little water. Agave is grown for tequila and fiber and thrives in desert land. The weed crabgrass, a C4 plant, tends to take over lawns in midsummer when cool-season C3 grasses such as bluegrass are stressed by heat, and this is why lawn care advice differs by season.

The leaf anatomy of C3 and C4 plants can be told apart under a microscope. In a C3 leaf the chloroplasts are found mainly in the mesophyll cells, and the cells around the veins have few. In a C4 leaf, the bundle sheath cells around the veins contain many large chloroplasts, giving the wreath pattern. CAM plants often have thick, fleshy leaves or stems with large vacuoles and a thick waxy cuticle, and sometimes a reduced surface area, as in the spines of a cactus, which are modified leaves, while the green stem does the photosynthesis.

Some plants are intermediate. Certain species show C3-C4 intermediate photosynthesis, in which a partial recycling of carbon dioxide released by photorespiration reduces losses. These plants are thought to resemble the evolutionary steps that led to full C4 photosynthesis. In the ice plant, Mesembryanthemum crystallinum, CAM can be switched on when the plant is stressed by salt or drought, which shows that the pathway can be regulated and is not fixed.
