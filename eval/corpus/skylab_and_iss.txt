Space stations: Skylab and the International Space Station

Skylab was the first American space station. It was built from the upper stage of a Saturn V, a hydrogen tank called the S-IVB, which engineers converted into a spacious two-deck workshop on the ground instead of fueling it for flight. Skylab was launched into orbit on May 14, 1973, atop a two-stage Saturn V, the last time that rocket flew. The station orbited about 435 kilometers above the Earth, weighed roughly 77,000 kilograms, and offered about 360 cubic meters of living and working space, far more than any Apollo spacecraft. It also carried a solar observatory, the Apollo Telescope Mount, which was designed to study the Sun in ultraviolet and X-ray light.

The launch was nearly a disaster. About a minute after liftoff, vibration tore off a thin shield that was meant to protect the workshop from micrometeoroids and also to shade it from the Sun, and in the process one of the two main solar arrays was ripped away. The other array was jammed shut by debris. Without its shield, the workshop's interior temperature climbed above 50 degrees Celsius, and with little electrical power available, the station was in serious trouble. NASA delayed the first crew launch by about ten days while engineers designed a fix.

The first crew, known as Skylab 2, launched on May 25, 1973, and consisted of Pete Conrad, Joseph Kerwin, and Paul Weitz. Conrad, who had earlier commanded Apollo 12, flew them up in an Apollo command module. They first tried to free the stuck solar array without success, then deployed a parasol-like sunshade through a small airlock in the workshop wall, which brought temperatures down to a livable level. Later in the mission, Conrad and Kerwin went outside and cut the metal strap that was holding the jammed array, and it swung open and began to supply power. The crew stayed for 28 days, and they returned on June 22, 1973.

The second crew, Skylab 3, launched on July 28, 1973, and included Alan Bean, Owen Garriott, and Jack Lousma. Bean had walked on the Moon on Apollo 12. They stayed for 59 days and returned on September 25, 1973. During their stay the crew installed a larger sunshade on the outside of the station, repaired leaking thrusters on their own spacecraft, and carried out a very large number of solar observations. They also ran an experiment in which two spiders, named Anita and Arabella, tried to spin webs in weightlessness. The spiders did manage to spin webs, although they were imperfect at first.

The third and last crew, Skylab 4, launched on November 16, 1973, and carried Gerald Carr, Edward Gibson, and William Pogue. All three were rookies. They spent 84 days in orbit, which set a record for American spaceflight, and returned on February 8, 1974. This crew observed Comet Kohoutek, which had been expected to be spectacular but proved disappointing to the naked eye. The crew's heavy workload led to a famous day of friction with mission control, after which the schedule was relaxed and the crew caught up. Gibson, a solar physicist, took thousands of images of the Sun, including a solar flare.

After the last crew left, Skylab was boosted to a higher orbit and left unoccupied, with the hope that a Space Shuttle would later visit it and raise its orbit again. The Shuttle was delayed, and increased solar activity expanded the upper atmosphere and dragged the station down faster than predicted. Skylab reentered over the Indian Ocean and Western Australia on July 11, 1979, scattering debris over sparsely populated land. No one was hurt, and a local shire in Australia famously fined NASA for littering.

The International Space Station, or ISS, is a much larger and longer-lived project. It was a partnership among five space agencies: NASA of the United States, Roscosmos of Russia, the European Space Agency, the Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency, and the Canadian Space Agency. Its assembly began on November 20, 1998, when the Russian-built module Zarya, funded by the United States, was launched on a Proton rocket from Kazakhstan. Two weeks later, on December 4, 1998, the Space Shuttle Endeavour carried up the American module Unity, which was joined to Zarya by astronauts during mission STS-88.

The station has been continuously occupied since November 2, 2000, when Expedition 1 arrived. That first crew was American astronaut William Shepherd and Russian cosmonauts Yuri Gidzenko and Sergei Krikalev. The earliest crews were only three people, but after the station had been expanded and commercial crew vehicles began flying, a normal crew grew to seven.

Modules were added over more than a decade. The American laboratory Destiny arrived in February 2001. The airlock Quest followed in 2001. The European laboratory Columbus was launched in February 2008, and the Japanese laboratory Kibo was brought up in several pieces in 2008 and 2009. Node 3, called Tranquility, and the seven-window observation dome called the Cupola arrived together in February 2010. The Russian segment included the Zvezda service module, which launched in July 2000 and provides living quarters and propulsion, and later modules such as Poisk and Rassvet.

The ISS orbits about 400 kilometers above the Earth, in an orbit inclined at 51.6 degrees to the equator so that it can be reached from Russian as well as American launch sites. It travels at about 28,000 kilometers per hour and circles the Earth roughly once every 92 minutes, so the crew sees about 16 sunrises and sunsets every day. The station's mass is about 420,000 kilograms. The pressurized volume is about 916 cubic meters, and the main truss and solar arrays span an area about the size of a football field, with an overall width of about 109 meters. Because the station sits in a very thin upper atmosphere, it slowly loses altitude, and it must be periodically reboosted by visiting cargo vehicles or by the Russian service module.

Scientific research on the ISS covers human physiology, materials science, fluid physics, biology, and Earth observation. Astronauts exercise about two hours each day to limit the loss of bone and muscle that occurs in microgravity. Long-duration studies, such as the twin study comparing astronaut Scott Kelly during a year in orbit to his identical brother on the ground, have explored how the body changes in space. Scott Kelly and Mikhail Kornienko spent 340 days on board in 2015 and 2016. The longest single spaceflight by an American is held by Frank Rubio, who stayed on the station for 371 days in 2022 and 2023 after his return ride was damaged by a coolant leak and his stay was extended.

Comparing the two stations highlights how much changed. Skylab was launched whole in one piece on a single rocket, whereas the ISS was assembled in orbit from dozens of modules over more than ten years. Skylab hosted three crews of three people for a total of 171 days of occupation, which is the sum of 28, 59, and 84 days. The ISS has been occupied without a break for more than two decades. Skylab's largest single stay was 84 days, while individual ISS stays commonly last about six months, and a few have lasted nearly a year or more.

Skylab was American alone, while the ISS joined former rivals. The idea of cooperation had earlier roots: in July 1975 an Apollo spacecraft docked with a Soviet Soyuz in orbit during the Apollo-Soyuz Test Project, and in the 1990s American Shuttles docked with the Russian station Mir. Experience from those missions made it possible for the ISS partners to share a single station.

Crew life on both stations required solutions to everyday problems. Skylab had a shower that proved awkward to use and a wardroom table where the crew could eat hot meals, which improved morale compared with earlier cold rations. On the ISS, water is recycled from humidity in the air and from urine, and the water recovery system now reclaims more than 90 percent of the water on board. Astronauts sleep in small private sleeping bags attached to walls, because there is no up or down.

The ISS is expected to continue operating into the 2030s, after which the plan is to guide it down to a controlled reentry over a remote area of the South Pacific. Commercial space stations are being developed to take over some of its research roles.

The Space Shuttle played a central role in building the ISS. Between 1998 and 2011 the Shuttle fleet carried most of the large American, European, and Japanese modules to orbit in its cargo bay and used a robotic arm to lift them into position. Astronauts then connected cables and pipes during spacewalks. The final Shuttle flight, by Atlantis, landed on July 21, 2011. After that, American astronauts relied on the Russian Soyuz spacecraft for transportation until May 30, 2020, when the SpaceX Crew Dragon spacecraft carried the astronauts Douglas Hurley and Robert Behnken to the station on the Demo-2 mission, the first crewed orbital flight from American soil since 2011. Cargo vehicles that regularly visit the station include the Russian Progress, the Cygnus, the Dragon cargo craft, and the Japanese HTV.

Another station, Mir, makes a useful comparison. The Soviet Union launched the core module of Mir on February 20, 1986, and added modules to it for a decade. It was occupied for most of its life and was brought down in a controlled reentry into the South Pacific on March 23, 2001. Cosmonaut Valeri Polyakov holds the record for the longest single spaceflight, 437 days aboard Mir from January 1994 to March 1995. That record is far longer than the 84 days of the longest Skylab crew, longer than the 340 days of Scott Kelly, and longer than the 371 days of Frank Rubio, who holds the American record. Mir was smaller than the ISS, weighing around 130,000 kilograms.

Life aboard Skylab used a layout that was familiar from the Earth. The workshop's lower deck held the crew quarters, a galley, and a shower, with private sleeping compartments for each crew member. The upper deck was a large open volume, almost like a gymnasium, where the astronauts discovered that they could fly from end to end and tumble in the air. A cylindrical airlock allowed them to move into the Apollo Telescope Mount and the outside. The Apollo Telescope Mount was a bundle of eight instruments mounted on a framework, with its own set of four solar panels arranged like a windmill. During the three crewed periods, Skylab astronauts took about 175,000 images of the Sun and recorded about 30 solar flares, giving scientists their first long continuous view of the Sun above the atmosphere.

A visit to the ISS today is a very different experience. A crew typically launches in a Soyuz or Crew Dragon and reaches the station in a few hours or one or two days, depending on the orbit chosen. The Russian Soyuz capsule has a standard flight lasting around six months, so the station crews are organized into numbered expeditions, and each one is named for the period between two crew changes. Expedition 1 was the first and began in November 2000. Several people are aboard at any time, normally some from the American segment and some from the Russian segment. The commander of an expedition is chosen from among its members, and the position often alternates between agencies.
