Project Mercury and Project Gemini: America's first steps into orbit

Project Mercury was the first American program to put people into space. Its goal was simple: orbit a crewed spacecraft around the Earth, observe how a person functions there, and recover both the person and the spacecraft safely. Seven men were selected in 1959 as the Mercury astronauts: Scott Carpenter, Gordon Cooper, John Glenn, Gus Grissom, Wally Schirra, Alan Shepard, and Deke Slayton. Slayton was grounded for a heart irregularity and never flew a Mercury mission. The program flew six crewed missions between 1961 and 1963. The capsule was a small cone just big enough for one person, who lay on a contoured couch facing the instrument panel with a heat shield at his back.

The first crewed flight was Freedom 7. Alan Shepard lifted off on May 5, 1961, on a Redstone rocket, flew a suborbital arc up to about 187 kilometers, and landed in the Atlantic Ocean after a flight of about fifteen minutes and twenty-two seconds. He did not go into orbit. His flight came just weeks after the Soviet cosmonaut Yuri Gagarin made a full orbit on April 12, 1961, so Shepard's short flight was seen as a catch-up step. Shepard was the first American in space, but not the first person.

The second flight, Liberty Bell 7, was also suborbital. Gus Grissom flew it on July 21, 1961, atop a Redstone. After splashdown the explosive hatch blew off before the recovery helicopter had hooked the capsule, and water poured in. Grissom escaped and nearly drowned when his suit began filling with water. The capsule sank in deep water and was not recovered until 1999, when a search team raised it from the Atlantic seabed. The flight lasted about fifteen minutes and thirty-seven seconds.

The first American orbital flight was Friendship 7 on February 20, 1962. John Glenn rode an Atlas rocket, which was strong enough to reach orbital speed, and completed three orbits in about four hours and fifty-five minutes. During the second orbit, a sensor falsely indicated that the heat shield had come loose. Controllers decided to leave the retrorocket package strapped on over the shield during reentry, in case it helped hold the shield in place, and Glenn watched burning fragments streak past his window. The heat shield was actually fine. Glenn became a national hero, and in 1998 he flew again on the Space Shuttle at the age of 77.

Aurora 7 followed on May 24, 1962, with Scott Carpenter, who also made three orbits. Carpenter was busy with experiments and used more fuel than planned for his attitude control thrusters. A misaligned spacecraft at retrofire made the capsule overshoot its planned landing area by about 400 kilometers, and he floated in his life raft for several hours before rescuers found him.

Sigma 7 was flown by Wally Schirra on October 3, 1962, and was a deliberately careful engineering flight. Schirra orbited six times in about nine hours and thirteen minutes and used a minimum of fuel, and he splashed down within a few kilometers of the waiting recovery ship.

The final Mercury mission, Faith 7, flew on May 15 and 16, 1963, with Gordon Cooper. He completed twenty-two orbits in a little over thirty-four hours, the first American flight to last more than a day. During the final orbits several systems failed, including the automatic controls and a power inverter. Cooper flew the reentry manually, using a wristwatch and sighting stars through the window, and landed very near the target ship. After this flight, NASA said Mercury had shown that people could live and work in space for a day and decided that the next step needed a more capable spacecraft.

Project Gemini bridged the gap between Mercury and Apollo. It used a two-person capsule launched by a Titan II rocket and was designed to practice the skills that a lunar landing would need: long-duration flight, rendezvous and docking, and working outside the spacecraft. The program flew ten crewed missions, from Gemini 3 in March 1965 to Gemini 12 in November 1966, plus two earlier uncrewed tests.

Gemini 3 launched on March 23, 1965, with Gus Grissom and John Young. It completed three orbits in about four hours and fifty-three minutes. Grissom nicknamed the capsule Molly Brown, a joking reference to his sunken Liberty Bell 7 capsule, and NASA officials disliked the name enough that later crews were told to use official designations only. Young also smuggled a corned beef sandwich aboard, which caused embarrassment when crumbs floated around the cabin.

Gemini 4 flew from June 3 to June 7, 1965, with James McDivitt and Edward White. On the third orbit, White opened his hatch and became the first American to walk in space, spending about twenty minutes outside, tethered to the spacecraft with a gold-coated umbilical line and moving with a hand-held oxygen jet gun. He enjoyed the experience so much that he was reluctant to come back inside. The mission lasted four days, much longer than any American flight so far.

Gemini 5 launched on August 21, 1965, and lasted almost eight days, ending on August 29, 1965. Gordon Cooper and Pete Conrad were on board. The mission proved that fuel cells could produce electricity for a long flight, and it showed that crews could survive in space for the duration of a lunar mission, which would be about eight days. A fuel cell pressure problem forced the crew to cancel a planned rendezvous exercise.

Gemini 7 took off on December 4, 1965, with Frank Borman and Jim Lovell, and stayed up for nearly fourteen days, landing on December 18, 1965. This was a deliberate endurance test to prove that people could survive a flight as long as a round trip to the Moon. The two men were in a space about the size of the front seat of a small car, and they took off their bulky suits for much of the flight, a procedure that had been tested for the first time on this mission. Gemini 7 held the duration record until 1970 when it was broken by a Soviet flight.

While Gemini 7 was still aloft, Gemini 6A launched on December 15, 1965, with Wally Schirra and Thomas Stafford. After the original Gemini 6 mission was canceled when its target vehicle failed, NASA reorganized the plan so that the Gemini 7 capsule became the rendezvous target. The two crewed spacecraft met in orbit on December 15, 1965, and flew in formation, at one point coming within about 30 centimeters of each other. They did not dock, since neither capsule carried a docking mechanism, but the maneuver showed that rendezvous in orbit worked.

Gemini 8 launched on March 16, 1966, with Neil Armstrong and David Scott. It achieved the first docking of two spacecraft in orbit, linking up with an uncrewed Agena target vehicle. Shortly afterward a stuck thruster on the Gemini spacecraft began to spin the combined vehicle faster and faster. Armstrong undocked, but the capsule continued to spin at almost one revolution per second, and he used the reentry control thrusters to stop the spin. The crew had to land early, in the Pacific Ocean, after only about ten hours in space. Armstrong's cool handling of the emergency helped win him the later command of Apollo 11.

Gemini 9A launched on June 3, 1966, with Thomas Stafford and Eugene Cernan. The original crew had been killed in a plane crash, and the replacements flew in their place. The planned docking failed because the shroud on the target vehicle had not fully separated. Cernan then attempted a spacewalk with a rocket-powered backpack, but his visor fogged and he became exhausted from fighting his suit, so the exercise was cut short after about two hours.

Gemini 10, flown in July 1966 by John Young and Michael Collins, docked with an Agena and used the Agena's engine to reach a higher orbit. Collins performed a spacewalk and retrieved a micrometeoroid experiment from a second target vehicle. Gemini 11 flew in September 1966 with Pete Conrad and Richard Gordon. They docked with their Agena within the first orbit, which was a rehearsal for a rapid rendezvous, and used the Agena's engine to reach a record altitude of about 1,369 kilometers. That remains the highest orbit ever reached by a crewed spacecraft apart from the Apollo flights to the Moon. Gordon also performed a spacewalk and rode the Agena like a bronco, tethered to the vehicle.

Gemini 12, the final flight, launched on November 11, 1966, with Jim Lovell and Buzz Aldrin, and ended on November 15, 1966. Earlier spacewalkers had struggled with fatigue and overheating, so engineers added handholds, foot restraints, and a better set of tasks, and trained the astronauts in a large underwater tank. Aldrin worked outside for three separate sessions totaling about five and a half hours, tightening bolts, cutting cables and attaching tethers without tiring. His success showed that spacewalking could be practical, and it made underwater training a standard method.

Taken together, the missions of Mercury and Gemini add up to sixteen crewed flights: six in Mercury and ten in Gemini. Of those, only the Mercury flights were flown by a single astronaut and the Gemini flights by two. By the time Gemini ended, American crews had practiced rendezvous, docking, long-duration flight, and spacewalking, all of which Apollo needed.

Several astronauts flew in more than one program. Gus Grissom flew Liberty Bell 7 and Gemini 3 and died in the Apollo 1 fire in January 1967. Wally Schirra flew Sigma 7, Gemini 6A, and later Apollo 7, the only astronaut to fly in all three of the programs. Gordon Cooper flew Faith 7 and Gemini 5. Pete Conrad flew Gemini 5, Gemini 11, Apollo 12, and Skylab. John Young flew Gemini 3, Gemini 10, Apollo 10, Apollo 16, and the first Space Shuttle mission.

The launch vehicles also differed. Mercury used the Redstone for its two suborbital hops and the more powerful Atlas for the four orbital flights. Gemini used the Titan II, which burned storable propellants and was reliable enough for crewed launches. The Agena target vehicle was launched by an Atlas.

The Soviet Union was ahead for most of the early period, which is the context for both programs. Yuri Gagarin made the first orbit of the Earth on April 12, 1961, aboard Vostok 1 in a flight of 108 minutes. Alexei Leonov performed the first spacewalk on March 18, 1965, from Voskhod 2, more than two months before Ed White's spacewalk on Gemini 4 on June 3, 1965. Valentina Tereshkova became the first woman in space in June 1963. The race with the Soviets gave NASA the push to speed up Gemini, which was planned as a program with just a short time between flights.

The Mercury capsule had a simple design. It was about 1.9 meters across at the base and about 2.9 meters long, and it weighed roughly 1,400 kilograms. A heat shield made of an ablative material that burned away during reentry protected the astronaut from the heat. After retrofire, the capsule came down on a parachute and landed in the sea. A small escape tower on top of the rocket could pull the capsule away from a failing booster, and it was tested several times. The Mercury astronauts also fought for a window and a hatch with explosive bolts, because the first design had neither; the window was added after Shepard and his colleagues insisted on being able to see out.

The Gemini capsule was a bigger and more advanced craft. It was roughly 5.8 meters long including its adapter section and weighed about 3,800 kilograms. It was equipped with ejection seats instead of an escape tower, since the Titan II used propellants that could burn violently, and it had a computer on board for the first time in an American crewed spacecraft. It also used fuel cells rather than batteries on longer flights, which made it possible to stay in space for days. Hatches opened outward like those of an aircraft canopy, so that an astronaut could step out into space, and the capsule landed in the ocean like the Mercury capsule, even though engineers had once planned a paraglider landing on land.

Rendezvous in space turned out to be counterintuitive. If a pilot tries to catch another spacecraft by speeding up, the pilot rises to a higher orbit and ends up moving slower and falling behind. Gemini crews learned to slow down to descend to a lower, faster orbit, catch up, and then raise their orbit again as they approached their target. Buzz Aldrin, who wrote his doctoral thesis on orbital rendezvous at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, became an expert on this and helped to design the procedures that Apollo used.

By the end of the program, the American total of time in space far exceeded the Soviet total. Gemini astronauts logged hundreds of hours of flight, and the Agena target vehicle, a small rocket stage that could be used as a docking target, proved that a spacecraft could change orbits after docking. The next step was Apollo, whose first crewed flight was planned for early 1967 but was cut short by the cabin fire in January of that year.
