The Voyager mission: two probes on a grand tour of the outer planets

The two Voyager spacecraft were launched by NASA in 1977 to take advantage of a rare alignment of the outer planets. Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune were arranged in such a way that a spacecraft could visit several of them in sequence, using the gravity of each planet to swing it onward to the next. This kind of alignment occurs only about once every 176 years. The idea of exploiting it, sometimes called the Grand Tour, was worked out in the 1960s, and the Voyager program was the realization of a scaled-down version of that plan. The mission was managed by the Jet Propulsion Laboratory in California.

Despite the numbering, Voyager 2 was launched first. It lifted off on August 20, 1977, from Cape Canaveral in Florida on a Titan IIIE rocket with a Centaur upper stage. Voyager 1 followed on September 5, 1977, on the same type of rocket. Voyager 1 was given a faster, shorter trajectory that would reach Jupiter and Saturn sooner, which is why it carries the lower number even though it left the Earth second. Each spacecraft had a mass of about 825 kilograms at launch, which is roughly the weight of a small car, and carried a large dish antenna 3.7 meters across for communicating with Earth.

Because the probes travel too far from the Sun to use solar panels, they get their electricity from radioisotope thermoelectric generators. These devices convert the heat released by the radioactive decay of plutonium-238 into electricity. The output drops slowly each year, about four watts annually, and engineers have had to switch off instruments one at a time to conserve power. Each probe carried a suite of eleven scientific instruments, including cameras, spectrometers, and detectors for charged particles, magnetic fields, and plasma waves. A scan platform allowed the cameras to be pointed at targets as the spacecraft hurtled past.

Voyager 1 reached Jupiter first, with its closest approach on March 5, 1979. It revealed that Jupiter's moon Io has active volcanoes, the first time that volcanism had been seen anywhere beyond the Earth. A navigation engineer named Linda Morabito spotted a plume rising above the edge of Io in an image taken to help the spacecraft find its way, and the discovery was a surprise because scientists had expected a frozen, dead moon. The spacecraft also found a faint ring around Jupiter and revealed the turbulent structure of the Great Red Spot.

Voyager 2 flew past Jupiter later, on July 9, 1979. It photographed the same system from a different angle, took more pictures of Europa, and confirmed that Io's volcanoes were still active months after Voyager 1 had seen them. Its images of Europa showed a smooth icy surface crossed by dark lines, hinting at a possible ocean beneath the ice.

Voyager 1 arrived at Saturn on November 12, 1980. Controllers chose to send the spacecraft close to Titan, Saturn's largest moon, because Titan has a thick atmosphere and it was one of the most intriguing bodies in the solar system. The flyby showed that Titan's atmosphere is mostly nitrogen with a layer of orange haze, hiding the surface. The close pass bent the spacecraft's path sharply northward, out of the plane of the planets, and ended its planetary tour. Voyager 1 could no longer visit Uranus or Neptune.

Voyager 2 reached Saturn on August 25, 1981. Its camera scan platform briefly stuck, which cost some images, but the problem was solved and the mission went on. It studied the rings in detail and used Saturn's gravity to bend its path toward Uranus.

Voyager 2 flew past Uranus on January 24, 1986, and it remains the only spacecraft to have visited that planet. It found that Uranus has a tilted magnetic field and a pale blue-green atmosphere with few visible features. The spacecraft discovered ten new moons and two new rings. It also took striking pictures of Miranda, a small moon with a patchwork of cliffs and grooves.

The last planet on the tour was Neptune. Voyager 2 made its closest approach on August 25, 1989, and passed only a few thousand kilometers above the planet's cloud tops. It discovered the Great Dark Spot, a storm system in the southern hemisphere, and found six new moons. Pictures of Neptune's large moon Triton revealed geysers of dark material rising from the surface, even though the temperature there is about minus 235 degrees Celsius. After Neptune, Voyager 2 headed south out of the plane of the planets.

Before leaving the planets behind, Voyager 1 took one more famous picture. On February 14, 1990, at the suggestion of the astronomer Carl Sagan, controllers turned the camera back toward the Sun and took a mosaic of the solar system's planets, from a distance of about six billion kilometers. In one frame, the Earth appears as a tiny pale point of light less than a pixel wide, caught in a beam of scattered sunlight. Sagan called it the Pale Blue Dot. After this series the cameras were turned off for good to save power and memory.

Each Voyager carries a message for any intelligent being who might find it, the Golden Record. This is a 12-inch gold-plated copper phonograph disc in a protective aluminum cover, accompanied by a cartridge and a stylus and instructions on how to play it. A committee chaired by Carl Sagan chose the contents. The record holds 115 images, a variety of natural sounds such as wind, thunder, and birdsong, greetings spoken in 55 languages, and about 90 minutes of music from many cultures, ranging from Bach and Beethoven to Chuck Berry and a Peruvian wedding song. It also carries electroencephalogram recordings of the brain waves of Ann Druyan, who later married Sagan. The cover is engraved with a diagram showing where the Earth is relative to fourteen pulsars, using their pulse rates as a kind of map.

In the decades after the planetary encounters, the probes continued outward on what is called the Interstellar Mission. Their instruments measured the solar wind, a stream of charged particles flowing from the Sun, and they were expected to detect the boundary where the solar wind gives way to the interstellar medium. This boundary is called the heliopause. It is not the edge of the solar system, since the Oort cloud of comets extends far beyond it, but it marks the limit of the Sun's magnetic and particle influence.

Voyager 1 crossed the heliopause on August 25, 2012, at a distance of about 121 astronomical units from the Sun, though the scientific team confirmed the crossing only in September 2013 after analyzing a sharp jump in the density of plasma. This made Voyager 1 the first human-made object to enter interstellar space. Voyager 2 followed on November 5, 2018, at a distance of about 119 astronomical units. Because Voyager 2 carried a working plasma instrument, its crossing provided the first direct measurements of the plasma density, speed, and temperature on the other side of the boundary.

The two spacecraft are traveling in different directions. Voyager 1 moves faster, at about 17 kilometers per second relative to the Sun, and heads out of the plane of the planets toward the constellation Ophiuchus. Voyager 2 travels at about 15 kilometers per second and heads southward, toward the constellation Pavo. The distances from Earth are so great that a radio signal needs more than twenty hours to reach Voyager 1, so commands sent to the spacecraft take a day to arrive and a reply takes another day. Messages are sent through the Deep Space Network, a set of large radio dishes in California, Spain, and Australia. Voyager 2 can be reached only by the Canberra dish in Australia for commands, since it is far in the southern sky.

The probes have been running for decades, far longer than planned, and they have suffered several failures. In 2023 a command sent to Voyager 2 accidentally pointed its antenna slightly away from the Earth, and contact was lost until a high-power signal from the Deep Space Network reset it. Voyager 1 began sending garbled data in late 2023 because of a faulty memory chip, and engineers repaired it in 2024 by moving the affected code to other locations in memory, working from a distance of more than 24 billion kilometers. Over the years the team has shut down cameras, heaters, and several science instruments. The remaining instruments are expected to keep working for only a few more years at best, after which the power will be too low to operate any of them. The spacecraft will continue to coast through interstellar space in silence. In about 40,000 years Voyager 1 will pass within roughly 1.6 light years of a star in the constellation Camelopardalis, and Voyager 2 will pass near the star Ross 248 around the same time.

It is helpful to compare the two probes directly. Voyager 1 visited two planets, Jupiter and Saturn, and the Saturn moon Titan. Voyager 2 visited four planets, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune, and is the only craft to see the last two up close. Voyager 1 was launched on September 5, 1977, while Voyager 2 was launched on August 20, 1977. Voyager 1 entered interstellar space in 2012; Voyager 2 did so in 2018. Voyager 1 is now the most distant human-made object, and Voyager 2 is slightly closer.

Some of the Voyager discoveries changed the way scientists think about other worlds. The active volcanoes of Io showed that tidal heating, produced by the squeezing of a moon by the gravity of its planet and sister moons, can power geology far from the Sun. The cracked ice of Europa began a long line of thought about oceans hidden under frozen crusts. The geysers on Triton showed that even a very cold moon can be active. The patterns in Saturn's rings, including thin braided strands and dark spokes, showed that the rings are shaped by tiny moons and electrical forces. Many of these results led directly to later missions, such as Galileo to Jupiter, Cassini to Saturn, and the plans for Europa Clipper.

Space probes and crewed missions have been compared in terms of cost and scientific return. The Voyager program cost roughly 865 million dollars through the Neptune encounter, a small sum compared with the cost of the Apollo program, which came to about 25.4 billion dollars at the time. Yet in terms of distance traveled, the Voyager probes have gone thousands of times farther than any person has ever journeyed from Earth. For comparison, the Apollo crews traveled about 400,000 kilometers to the Moon, while Voyager 1 is now more than 24 billion kilometers away.

Voyager was not the first mission to the outer planets. Pioneer 10 was launched on March 2, 1972, and became the first spacecraft to cross the asteroid belt and the first to fly past Jupiter, on December 3, 1973. Pioneer 11 launched on April 5, 1973, flew by Jupiter in December 1974, and then became the first spacecraft to visit Saturn, in September 1979. Both Pioneers carried a metal plaque showing a man and a woman and a map of the Sun's position among pulsars, designed by Carl Sagan and Frank Drake, who were assisted by Linda Salzman Sagan. These probes tested whether it was safe to pass through the asteroid belt and the intense radiation near Jupiter, and their data helped the Voyager engineers harden their spacecraft.

Later missions built on the discoveries by Voyager. The Galileo spacecraft was launched on October 18, 1989, by the Space Shuttle Atlantis and went into orbit around Jupiter in December 1995, spending about eight years studying the planet and its moons. The Cassini spacecraft was launched on October 15, 1997, entered orbit around Saturn on July 1, 2004, and carried the Huygens probe, which landed on Titan in January 2005. Cassini ended its mission on September 15, 2017, by plunging into Saturn's atmosphere. The New Horizons spacecraft launched on January 19, 2006, flew past Jupiter for a gravity assist in 2007, and made the first close flyby of Pluto on July 14, 2015, before visiting the Kuiper belt object Arrokoth on January 1, 2019. Unlike Voyager, which only flew past, Galileo and Cassini orbited their targets for years.

The communications system of the Voyager probes is both fragile and remarkable. A transmitter on each spacecraft emits only about 23 watts, which is roughly the power of a refrigerator light bulb, and by the time the signal crosses billions of kilometers it is extraordinarily faint. Large dishes on the ground, 34 and 70 meters in diameter, collect it. Data rates fell from about 115,000 bits per second at Jupiter to only a couple of hundred bits per second today. The onboard computers have a combined memory of only about 69 kilobytes, millions of times smaller than a modern phone, and they were programmed in assembly language. The tape recorder on each probe used magnetic tape that could store about 100 images at a time.

The mission has also been a human story. Ed Stone served as the project scientist from the start of the program in 1972 until 2022, a span of fifty years, which is one of the longest tenures in the history of space science. Many of the original engineers retired long ago, and teams have repeatedly had to rediscover how the spacecraft work by reading old documents. Susan Dodd has managed the project in recent years and has led the efforts to keep the probes alive as their power and fuel decline.
